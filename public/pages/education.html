Air Pollution Basics

Start with the foundations: what air pollution is, what kinds of pollutants people are exposed to, and why local air quality matters for health, schools, and communities in Metro Atlanta.

Core Concepts

Air pollution refers to harmful substances in the air that can affect human health, ecosystems, and quality of life. These substances include both particles and gases. Some pollutants are emitted directly, such as vehicle exhaust or industrial emissions, while others form later through chemical reactions in the atmosphere.

For communities, air pollution is not just an environmental topic. It is also a public health issue because exposure can affect breathing, heart health, school attendance, and long-term well-being. This makes local monitoring especially important in places where traffic, industrial activity, and land use vary from neighborhood to neighborhood.

ParticlesPM1, PM2.5, PM10
GasesNO₂, O₃, CO, SO₂
ExposureHealth + environment
Source: U.S. EPA, Criteria Air Pollutants
High health concern

PM2.5 refers to airborne particles that are 2.5 micrometers or smaller in diameter. Because these particles are so small, they can travel deep into the lungs and contribute to asthma attacks, inflammation, cardiovascular strain, and other chronic health risks.

PM2.5 often comes from combustion-related sources such as cars, trucks, power generation, and smoke. In Metro Atlanta, PM2.5 is especially important because traffic corridors and regional weather conditions can combine to produce noticeable differences in exposure across time and place.

2.5 µmVery small particles
TrafficMajor local source
HealthLungs + heart
Source: EPA PM2.5 Basics; CDC Air Pollution and Health

Pollution is not distributed evenly. Communities near highways, heavy traffic, industrial zones, or construction areas often face greater exposure than areas farther away from those sources. Wind direction, temperature, and urban form can also affect how pollution moves and where it settles.

This is one reason neighborhood-level data matters. Citywide averages can make air quality seem moderate even when certain schools or residential areas are experiencing much higher local exposure.

Graphs & Trends

Learn how to read pollution trends over time and make sense of changing values, spikes, and repeated patterns in local air quality data.

Reading Data Over Time

A graph helps users see how pollution values change over time rather than focusing on just one number. Trend lines are useful because they reveal whether conditions are increasing, decreasing, or remaining relatively stable during a chosen period.

This matters for air quality because pollution often follows repeating rhythms linked to commuting, weather, school schedules, or seasonal conditions. A graph allows people to compare those rhythms and ask more meaningful questions.

Source: AirNow Educational Materials; NOAA Data Literacy Resources

A spike is a sudden increase in pollution values. Some spikes are one-time events, while others happen repeatedly and point to predictable causes. For example, weekday traffic can create repeated morning and afternoon increases, while wildfire smoke may cause a regional rise over a shorter period.

Interpreting spikes requires context. Looking at date, time, weather, and nearby activities helps users move from “What happened?” to “Why might this have happened?”

TrafficRecurring pattern
SmokeRegional event
ConstructionLocalized increase
Source: EPA Air Sensor Guidebook; PurpleAir Interpretation Guidance

Comparison is what turns graphs into analysis. Looking across weekdays and weekends, different months, or different schools can reveal whether patterns are tied to traffic, weather, regional smoke, or local activity.

This helps students and educators use graphs not just as visual displays, but as tools for building evidence and drawing conclusions.

Source: Data Literacy Project; EPA Student Air Quality Resources

Mapping Pollution

Understand how maps reveal spatial differences in air quality and why location matters when interpreting environmental data.

Location and Patterns

Maps make pollution data easier to connect to real places. A graph may show that pollution increased, but a map helps answer where that increase happened and which nearby features might help explain it.

In educational settings, maps also make data more meaningful because students can relate patterns to familiar schools, roads, parks, or neighborhoods.

Source: ESRI GIS Education Resources; EPA Community Air Mapping Guidance

GIS allows users to compare data across locations and identify hotspots, meaning places where pollution is consistently or unusually high. These hotspots are often associated with major roads, industrial areas, and dense traffic corridors.

This makes spatial comparison especially useful for local air quality education because it shows that one neighborhood’s experience may differ significantly from another’s.

HighwaysFrequent hotspots
SchoolsImportant comparison points
GISShows where patterns happen
Source: ESRI Spatial Thinking Resources; EPA EJScreen

Broad averages can hide local inequality. Neighborhood-level mapping helps communities, educators, and students see where pollution burdens may be concentrated and ask why those differences exist.

This supports stronger questions about environmental justice, land use, transportation, and public health. Local mapping does not solve those issues on its own, but it helps make them visible.

Source: EPA Environmental Justice Resources; WHO Urban Health Guidance

Research Projects

Use air quality data to support inquiry, observation, comparison, and student-led research.

Inquiry and Investigation

A good research question is specific, observable, and answerable with evidence. For example, a student might ask whether weekday mornings show higher PM2.5 than weekends near a school, or whether one location consistently records more spikes than another.

Questions like these are useful because they invite comparison and make it easier to connect claims to actual data rather than relying on assumptions.

Source: National Science Teaching Association Inquiry Guidance

Strong projects do not depend on one number alone. They combine evidence from graphs, maps, dates, context, and nearby conditions such as traffic, weather, land use, or school schedules.

This helps students support claims more clearly. For example, a graph may show a spike, while a map and local context help explain whether that spike was likely linked to road proximity, smoke, or another factor.

Source: Data Literacy Project; NOAA Climate and Data Education Tools

Inquiry-based learning encourages students to ask questions, interpret evidence, develop claims, and reflect on uncertainty. Environmental data is especially useful for this because it connects science to real community issues that students can see, discuss, and investigate.

In a project setting, inquiry helps transform the module from passive reading into active learning. Students begin to think like investigators, using local data to understand their environment more deeply.

Source: NSTA Inquiry-Based Learning; EPA Student Environmental Education Resources
